## 1 — Introduction & Financial Terms and Concepts

The opening presentation maps financial instruments, participants, and purposes: currencies, equities, loans and bonds, commodities, mortgages and securitizations, derivatives, exchanges, and OTC markets. It separates corporate hedging, market making, and proprietary trading, then connects quantitative methods to pricing, risk management, and strategy development. Cross-currency funding and hedging illustrate why a superficially attractive interest-rate difference does not settle the economics of a trade. Alpha and beta appear through the return relationship \(R_a=\alpha+\beta R_b\). Dealers manage inventories of exposures and Greeks, not merely price forecasts. Risk-preference examples contrast uncertain and certain gains or losses. Project illustrations include Monte Carlo delta estimation and Kalman filtering of asynchronous broker quotes. The optional MIT activity here is constructing a personal financial glossary. Assumption awareness is the principal lesson: historical extrapolation and simplified models need scrutiny, and price, hedge, and execution problems differ. [Slides, pp. 3–22](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-s096-topics-in-mathematics-with-applications-in-finance-fall-2013/10802d98a8af7ca93a024eac0cb0dded_MIT18_S096F13_lecnote1.pdf).

**Suggested exercise:** Explain the distinct exposures of a borrower, a dealer, and a speculator holding the same bond.

## 2 — Linear Algebra

Matrices are introduced as transformations and as financial data arrays indexed by companies and dates. The notes develop eigenvalues and eigenvectors, diagonalization, the spectral properties of real symmetric matrices, singular value decomposition, reduced SVD, and the Perron–Frobenius theorem. Two anchors are \(Av=\lambda v\) and \(A=U\Sigma V^\top\). Symmetry guarantees real eigenvalues and an orthonormal eigenbasis; SVD also handles rectangular matrices and separates input from output directions. Singular values arise from eigenvalues of \(A^\top A\), enabling comparison of directions and scales in market data. Perron–Frobenius is stated for matrices with strictly positive entries, yielding a unique dominant eigenvalue and positive eigenvector; the condition should not be silently generalized. Financial applications to factor extraction and transition matrices are study connections to later lectures, rather than a complete portfolio model taught here. [Notes, pp. 1–5](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-s096-topics-in-mathematics-with-applications-in-finance-fall-2013/bf77e900565f6487e3f46ba38dcc6dd6_MIT18_S096F13_lecnote2.pdf).

**Suggested exercise:** Compute an SVD of a small return matrix and interpret its largest singular direction.

## 3 — Probability Theory

The notes review discrete mass functions, continuous densities, expectations, cumulative distributions, mutual versus pairwise independence, and lack of correlation. Normal and lognormal distributions lead into change of variables and exponential-family representations, with Poisson and exponential distributions named as further examples. Moment-generating functions encode moments through \(M_X(t)=E[e^{tX}]\); distribution identification needs existence conditions, and equal moments alone need not identify a distribution. The weak law of large numbers is proved using a variance bound, then an MGF argument establishes \(\sqrt n(\bar X_n-\mu)\Rightarrow N(0,\sigma^2)\). The presented CLT assumes i.i.d. variables and an MGF in addition to mean and variance; the general finite-variance CLT needs weaker conditions. Financial applications include positive stock-price models through lognormality and uncertainty in estimated means. Neither theorem establishes independence or Gaussian one-period market returns. For accuracy, a lognormal MGF fails for positive arguments; the PDF's broader claim about all nonzero arguments should not be repeated. [Notes, pp. 1–6](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-s096-topics-in-mathematics-with-applications-in-finance-fall-2013/94dc1d08ef12065198102ae23b2e4a8f_MIT18_S096F13_lecnote3.pdf).

## 5 — Stochastic Processes I

The lecture treats a process as time-indexed random variables or a distribution over paths. Discrete-time random walks introduce independent stationary increments, CLT scaling, and boundary-hitting probabilities. Finite Markov chains add transition matrices, multi-step transitions, and stationary distributions; strictly positive transitions ensure uniqueness and convergence in the theorem presented. Martingales formalize fair games through \(E[X_{t+1}\mid\mathcal F_t]=X_t\). Stopping times must depend only on observed information. The weak optional-stopping result \(E[X_\tau]=E[X_0]\) assumes a bounded stopping time, and the notes explicitly challenge strategies that ignore this requirement. Financial relevance includes stock-path modeling, threshold events, and evaluating claims that timing alone manufactures expected gains. The notes acknowledge that a simple random walk is an inaccurate stock-price model. Use column-stochastic matrices consistently with the document's convention; many other texts transpose that convention. [Notes, pp. 1–7](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-s096-topics-in-mathematics-with-applications-in-finance-fall-2013/f5784e4facf3de690210d17c97358eba_MIT18_S096F13_lecnote5.pdf).

**Suggested exercise:** Compare a bounded stopping rule with “stop upon first reaching a profit target” and identify the theorem's missing condition.

## 6 — Regression Analysis

The slides build multiple linear regression, ordinary least squares, Gauss–Markov optimality, generalized least squares, normal-model distribution theory, likelihood estimation, and robust M-estimation. Linearity concerns parameters, so polynomial, Fourier, and lagged-response predictors can still form linear models. With full column rank, \(\hat\beta=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y\); with known positive-definite error covariance \(\Omega\), GLS uses \(\hat\beta=(X^\top\Omega^{-1}X)^{-1}X^\top\Omega^{-1}y\). Classical Gauss–Markov assumes zero-mean, equal-variance, uncorrelated errors; normality is an additional assumption for exact Gaussian inference. General covariance and contaminated distributions motivate alternatives. The workflow includes choosing a response scale and estimator criterion, fitting, checking assumptions, and revising the model. Financial applications such as return-factor estimation and forecasting are study connections. A fitted relation alone does not establish causal interpretation. [Slides, especially pp. 2–7 and subsequent estimation sections](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-s096-topics-in-mathematics-with-applications-in-finance-fall-2013/fc0c08db6b497cc3bd09020aec39f9b5_MIT18_S096F13_lecnote6.pdf).

**Suggested exercise:** Fit the same return-factor model with OLS and an outlier-resistant loss, then inspect the residuals.

## 7 — Value At Risk (VAR) Models

Kenneth Abbott's application lecture compares variance–covariance, Monte Carlo, and historical simulation methods, starting from one-asset examples and extending to multiple exposures. Interest-rate duration/PV01, equity and commodity exposure, credit-spread sensitivity, nonlinear pricing, and cross-market correlation shape the calculation. In cleaned loss notation, \(\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(L)=\inf\{\ell:P(L\leq\ell)\geq\alpha\}\): a quantile threshold is distinct from average severity beyond that threshold. The slides use the worst one-percent region as a motivating example. Implementation spans market-data selection, coverage periods, position data, sensitivities or repricing, aggregation, reports, and backtests. A standalone number is insufficient; the presentation explicitly pairs VaR with stress testing and scenario analysis. Distributional fit, stability of correlations, representative historical windows, and treatment of nonlinear instruments are method-specific assumptions. Timing and recognition of P&L complicate validation. [Slides, pp. 4–7, 46–47, 74–76, 102–103](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-s096-topics-in-mathematics-with-applications-in-finance-fall-2013/8c9f9d321f7c8bb34b289f178f7cfc32_MIT18_S096F13_lecnote7.pdf).

**Suggested exercise:** Compare Gaussian and historical VaR for one portfolio and explain why identical VaR can conceal different tail losses.

## 8 — Time Series Analysis I

This lecture distinguishes strict from covariance stationarity, introduces autocovariance/autocorrelation and the Wold representation, and develops ARMA and ARIMA models. Covariance stationarity requires time-invariant mean and variance and covariance depending only on lag. A compact model is \(\phi(L)X_t=\theta(L)\varepsilon_t\); differencing produces the ARIMA extension \(\phi(L)(1-L)^dX_t=\theta(L)\varepsilon_t\). The notes distinguish deterministic trends from stochastic trends and show why a random walk is nonstationary. Estimation includes conditional and full Gaussian likelihoods, followed by AIC, BIC, and Hannan–Quinn order selection. Unit-root testing includes Dickey–Fuller and references to augmented variants; these begin with nonstationarity as the null. Financial study applications include separating price-level trends from return dynamics and designing forecasts. White-noise innovation assumptions, root conditions, and appropriate differencing must precede interpretation of fitted coefficients. [Slides, pp. 3–5 and 21–32](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-s096-topics-in-mathematics-with-applications-in-finance-fall-2013/1926c83ecd7ea700f7cb63914c6d7c0f_MIT18_S096F13_lecnote8.pdf).

**Suggested exercise:** Compare the autocorrelation of simulated random-walk levels with their first differences.

## 9 — Volatility Modeling

The slides distinguish historical volatility measurement from prediction and mention implied volatility as a separate input. Log returns, annualization, geometric Brownian motion, Poisson jump diffusion, ARCH, and GARCH provide increasingly flexible descriptions. GARCH(1,1) obeys \(\sigma_t^2=\omega+\alpha\varepsilon_{t-1}^2+\beta\sigma_{t-1}^2\), and its finite long-run variance is \(\omega/(1-\alpha-\beta)\) when parameters satisfy the relevant positivity and \(\alpha+\beta<1\) conditions. Conditional variance can vary even when unconditional moments remain stationary. Gaussian conditional likelihood estimation is followed by checks on standardized residuals and their squares, normality diagnostics, and information criteria. Financial applications include changing risk forecasts and models of asset-price dynamics; jump diffusion addresses moves a continuous diffusion cannot represent. Historical standard deviation, conditional forecast variance, and option-implied volatility answer different questions. Square-root time scaling needs justified dependence assumptions. [Slides, pp. 3–5 and 22–27](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-s096-topics-in-mathematics-with-applications-in-finance-fall-2013/32f868169964ba3cf5015de880cf2172_MIT18_S096F13_lecnote9.pdf).

**Suggested exercise:** Fit a GARCH model and check whether standardized squared residuals retain serial dependence.

## 10 — Regularized Pricing and Risk Models

Ivan Masyukov connects bonds, swaps, discount factors, yield curves, and risk projection to inverse problems. Historical PCA can produce unstable hedge coefficients, especially with short windows and small singular values. A proposed structural assumption is smooth movement of forward rates. The lecture then studies HJM volatility-surface calibration, B-spline representations, weighted fitting, gradient penalties, and Tikhonov regularization. A simplified anchor is \(\hat x=\arg\min_x\{\|Ax-y\|^2+\lambda^2\|x\|^2\}\), yielding \((A^\top A+\lambda^2I)^{-1}A^\top y\). The full surface problem includes additional weights and regularity matrices. Small singular values amplify quote noise, so exact repricing of benchmarks does not guarantee stable portfolio valuation or hedging. Financial use is robust calibration and interpretable hedge allocation across liquid instruments. Smoothness and penalty strength introduce modeling choices, not information established by market quotes alone. [Slides, pp. 17–26 and 36–48](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-s096-topics-in-mathematics-with-applications-in-finance-fall-2013/0d3edf3e95c22b1b217a0ec8b59d8ecc_MIT18_S096F13_lecnote10.pdf).

**Suggested exercise:** Perturb quotes in an ill-conditioned calibration and compare parameter and portfolio-price sensitivity across penalty strengths.

## 11 — Time Series Analysis II

The multivariate extension introduces vector moments and cross-lag covariance, multivariate Wold representation, vector autoregressions, companion-form representation, least-squares estimation, likelihood, order selection, and asymptotic inference. The central model is \(X_t=c+\sum_{j=1}^p\Phi_jX_{t-j}+\eta_t\). Cross-series lag coefficients capture predictive relationships absent from separate univariate models. A VAR(p) can be stacked into VAR(1), making stability a matrix-eigenvalue question. An important result is that equation-by-equation OLS equals GLS for an unrestricted model with a common regressor matrix, even when contemporaneous innovations correlate. Gaussian innovations additionally give the conditional maximum-likelihood interpretation. The notes use Kronecker products and column-vectorization to demonstrate this equivalence. Financial study applications include joint forecasting and dependence among market variables. Stability, sufficient observations relative to parameter count, and the unrestricted/common-regressor conditions constrain the result; predictive dependence alone is not causal identification. [Slides, pp. 2–11 and 18–25](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-s096-topics-in-mathematics-with-applications-in-finance-fall-2013/e4cd8dc88db55a6049b4d2aa3e4a1fb1_MIT18_S096F13_lecnote11.pdf).

**Suggested exercise:** Fit a two-variable VAR and compare component-wise OLS with the corresponding unrestricted GLS fit.

## 12 — Time Series Analysis III

Cointegration allows nonstationary components to have stationary linear combinations: \(\beta^\top X_t\sim I(0)\) despite each component being I(1). Cointegrated VARs lead to vector error-correction models, with long-run adjustment separated from short-run differences. The lecture also develops linear state-space observation and transition equations, Kalman filtering, forecasting, smoothing, and likelihood-based parameter estimation. One clean representation is \(y_t=Z_ts_t+\varepsilon_t,\;s_{t+1}=T_ts_t+R_t\eta_t\). Under the stated linear Gaussian setup, recursive conditional means and covariances summarize the filtering distributions; smoothing uses later observations to revise earlier state estimates. Cointegration rank and vector normalization affect interpretation. Financial study connections include equilibrium spreads and estimating latent quantities from noisy quotes; these are applications of the methods, not evidence of profitable trades. Crucially, smoothing has information unavailable to a real-time trading decision. [Slides, pp. 3–14 and 15–38](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-s096-topics-in-mathematics-with-applications-in-finance-fall-2013/2878a31dc5f908839d3c66f61f41f85c_MIT18_S096F13_lecnote12.pdf).

**Suggested exercise:** Contrast filtered and smoothed latent-price estimates and mark which observations each estimate uses.

## 13 — Commodity Models

Alexander Eydeland values operational flexibility, beginning with contango storage trades and expanding to storage options and constrained optimization. Storage capacity, injection/withdrawal limits, and inventory couple decisions across dates; simulation and stochastic control provide solution approaches. A calendar-spread payoff is \(\max(F_j-F_i-C,0)\), while a merchant plant earns a spark-spread payoff \(\max(P_{\rm power}-hP_{\rm fuel}-C_{\rm variable},0)\), with consistent heat-rate units. Electricity prices exhibit spikes, mean reversion, heavy tails, regime changes, and complex fuel-price dependence. The presentation compares diffusion, jump, stochastic-volatility, convenience-yield, and term-structure models, explaining why non-storability obstructs the usual spot-based replication argument. A hybrid bid-stack model instead links price to demand, fuel costs, and outages: \(P_t=S_{\rm bid}(D_t)\). Calibration must preserve joint dependence and higher moments relevant to spread options and real assets. [Slides, pp. 5–20 and 27–42](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-s096-topics-in-mathematics-with-applications-in-finance-fall-2013/7ba72c0dc888b352d301736029456ee9_MIT18_S096F13_lecnote13.pdf).

**Suggested exercise:** Value a simple storage schedule, then add a capacity constraint and explain the change in feasible hedges.
